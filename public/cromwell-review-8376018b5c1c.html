Tax Law Attorney in Maryland — Civil & Criminal Tax Representation

CIVIL TAX CONTROVERSY

Most tax problems start with an IRS notice: an audit, a balance assessed from an unfiled return, or a collection notice. From there, the IRS moves through an escalating set of collection tools, each with its own notice requirements and its own window to respond.

Audits. Representation during an IRS or Maryland tax audit, from the initial document request through appeal of a proposed adjustment.

Liens. A federal tax lien is the government's legal claim against property securing an unpaid balance. Liens are public record and can complicate selling or refinancing property well beyond the dollar amount involved.

Levies and wage garnishment. A levy is the actual seizure of property or funds; a wage garnishment is a continuous levy on a paycheck. The IRS must send a Final Notice of Intent to Levy first — the most important window to act before enforcement begins.

Installment agreements. A structured monthly payment plan that stops active enforcement as long as payments stay current.

Offers in compromise. An agreement to settle a tax debt for less than the full balance, available when the full amount is genuinely not collectible given income, expenses, and asset equity.

CRIMINAL TAX DEFENSE

Most tax disputes are entirely civil. They become criminal exposure in narrower circumstances — willful failure to file over multiple years, deliberate underreporting, or affirmative acts to conceal income or assets. Recognizing that line, and responding to it early, is often what determines whether a matter stays civil.

Landlord-Tenant Attorney in Maryland — Eviction, Lease, and Property Representation

Cromwell Attorneys & Advisors represents landlords, property managers, and rental property owners throughout Frederick, Montgomery, Carroll, and Howard Counties, Maryland, handling the full range of landlord-tenant matters with the precision of an attorney who also understands the financial side of the business.

EVICTIONS AND FAILURE TO PAY RENT

Maryland does not allow landlords to remove a tenant on their own — every eviction goes through District Court, from the initial complaint through judgment and, when necessary, a warrant of restitution. Failure-to-pay-rent actions are the most common eviction basis, but breach-of-lease and holdover proceedings follow their own procedural tracks, and filing under the wrong one is a common way cases get dismissed. Maryland law also gives tenants a right of redemption in failure-to-pay-rent cases, letting them stop an eviction by paying what's owed before it's carried out — a step that surprises landlords who assume a judgment ends the matter.

LEASE DISPUTES AND LEASE DRAFTING

A lease is only as strong as the clauses a Maryland court will actually enforce. Self-help eviction clauses, late fees above the statutory cap, blanket waivers of the security deposit statute, and confession-of-judgment clauses are all common in generic templates and largely unenforceable in Maryland. Cromwell Attorneys & Advisors reviews and drafts leases built to hold up, and litigates disputes over lease terms, renewals, and violations.

SECURITY DEPOSIT DISPUTES

Maryland's security deposit statute is specific and unforgiving of shortcuts: a 45-day return deadline, a written itemized list of deductions, and move-in inspection documentation. Missing any of these — even without bad intent — can expose a landlord to treble damages and the tenant's attorney's fees, regardless of whether the underlying deduction was fair.

HABITABILITY, REPAIRS, AND RENT ESCROW

Maryland's rent escrow process lets a tenant pay rent to the court instead of the landlord over alleged serious defects, provided the landlord had proper notice and a fair opportunity to repair. Common defenses include improper notice, conditions that aren't serious enough, repairs already made, or tenant-caused damage — all of which depend on documentation built before the dispute, not after.

HOLDOVER AND BREACH-OF-LEASE PROCEEDINGS

Representation when a tenant remains in possession after a lease ends or is violated, including holdover proceedings and breach-of-lease actions distinct from nonpayment cases.

ENTITY FORMATION AND STRUCTURE

Choosing between operating as a sole proprietorship and forming an LLC is a liability decision first and a tax decision second — and the two are easy to conflate. Bankruptcy Attorney in Maryland — Personal and Business Debt Relief

Cromwell Attorneys & Advisors represents individuals and business owners across Maryland evaluating or pursuing bankruptcy relief under the U.S. Bankruptcy Code, aimed at finding the most workable path forward given both the legal process and the client's actual financial picture.

CHAPTER 7: LIQUIDATION

Chapter 7 is often called "liquidation" bankruptcy, though most individual filers don't lose significant property — Maryland and federal exemptions protect a meaningful amount of equity in a home, vehicle, and personal belongings. A trustee reviews the filer's assets, sells anything that isn't exempt to pay creditors, and most remaining unsecured debt — credit cards, medical bills, personal loans — is discharged. The process is comparatively fast, often concluding within a few months. Eligibility depends on a "means test" comparing household income to the Maryland median for a household of the same size.

CHAPTER 13: REORGANIZATION

Chapter 13 is a repayment plan, typically spanning three to five years, built around the filer's actual income and expenses. It's often the better fit for someone who doesn't qualify for Chapter 7, who has non-exempt property they want to keep, or who is behind on a mortgage and wants a structured way to catch up while keeping the house. At the end of a successful plan, remaining eligible unsecured debt is discharged.

BUSINESS BANKRUPTCY

Business owners facing unmanageable debt have their own set of options, including Chapter 7 liquidation of a business entity or reorganization under Chapter 11 (including a streamlined path for smaller businesses) aimed at keeping the business operating while restructuring its debts. The right path depends heavily on whether the business is viable going forward or whether the goal is an orderly wind-down.

WHAT BANKRUPTCY DOESN'T ERASE

Certain debts generally survive bankruptcy regardless of chapter, including most tax debt, recent domestic support obligations, and most student loans absent a hard-to-obtain showing of hardship. Understanding what actually gets discharged is often the difference between bankruptcy solving the problem and simply delaying it.

The Maryland Eviction Timeline: A Step-by-Step Guide for Landlords

Landlord/Tenant — Maryland Eviction Process (anchor pillar)

For a Maryland landlord, a tenant who stops paying rent or violates a lease isn't just a cash-flow problem — it's a legal process with specific steps, specific deadlines, and specific ways to get it wrong. Maryland does not allow landlords to remove a tenant on their own, even after a lease has clearly been broken. Every eviction has to go through District Court, and skipping or rushing a step is the single most common reason a case gets delayed or dismissed. Here's what that process actually looks like, county by county nuances included.

Step 1: Confirm the Legal Basis for Eviction

Maryland recognizes a few distinct grounds for eviction, and each one follows a different procedural track:

  • Failure to Pay Rent — the most common basis, filed once rent is past due.
  • Breach of Lease — used when a tenant violates a specific lease term other than nonpayment (unauthorized occupants, property damage, illegal activity).
  • Holdover / Tenant Holding Over — used when a tenant remains in the unit after their lease has expired or after a valid notice to vacate.

Filing under the wrong category is a common, avoidable mistake — it's the fastest way to have a case thrown out at the first hearing and lose weeks starting over.

Step 2: File the Complaint in District Court

The complaint is filed in the District Court for the county where the rental property sits — Frederick, Montgomery, Carroll, and Howard Counties each have their own District Court location and, in practice, their own scheduling quirks and local expectations, even though the underlying state law is the same. Montgomery County adds a wrinkle most landlords don't expect: the county's own landlord-tenant licensing rules can affect whether a case is allowed to proceed at all if the rental unit isn't properly licensed.

Step 3: The Hearing

Maryland District Court hearings for landlord-tenant cases move fast, often with a high volume of cases heard in a single session. Landlords should come prepared with the lease, a rent ledger showing the payment history, and any written notices sent to the tenant. Judges expect documentation, not just an account of what happened.

Step 4: Judgment and the Tenant's Right of Redemption

In a Failure to Pay Rent case, Maryland law gives the tenant a right of redemption — the ability to stop the eviction by paying the full amount owed (plus court costs) before the eviction is actually carried out. This is one of the most misunderstood parts of the process: winning a judgment in court does not necessarily mean the matter is over. Landlords are frequently surprised when a tenant pays at the last possible moment and the case effectively resets.

Step 5: The Warrant of Restitution

If the tenant doesn't exercise the right of redemption and doesn't leave voluntarily, the landlord's next step is requesting a warrant of restitution, which authorizes the county sheriff to remove the tenant. This is also where landlords most often try to shortcut the process — changing locks, removing belongings, or shutting off utilities without a warrant — which is illegal self-help eviction in Maryland and can expose the landlord to liability regardless of how clearly the tenant was in the wrong.

What Gets Landlord Cases Dismissed

  • Improper or incomplete notice to the tenant before filing
  • Filing under the wrong ground for eviction
  • Missing or incomplete rent ledger documentation
  • Attempting self-help eviction (changing locks, removing belongings) before a warrant is issued
  • Skipping county-specific licensing requirements, particularly in Montgomery County

The Takeaway

Maryland's eviction process rewards landlords who document everything and follow the sequence exactly — and it punishes shortcuts. An attorney who handles landlord-side matters regularly can usually spot the procedural issue that would get a case dismissed before it happens, which saves far more time than it costs.

5 Lease Clauses That Won't Hold Up in Maryland Court (And What to Use Instead)

Landlord/Tenant — Leases and Prevention

Word count · ~950
Status · Draft, pending Stephen's review

A lease is only as strong as the clauses a court will actually enforce. Landlords frequently copy lease language from templates, prior landlords, or generic online forms without knowing that Maryland law overrides or restricts several common provisions. A dispute is the worst time to discover a clause you were counting on doesn't hold up. Here are five of the most common problem clauses in Maryland residential leases — and what to use instead.

1. Self-Help Eviction / "Landlord May Re-Enter and Remove" Clauses

Some leases still include language allowing the landlord to change the locks, remove a tenant's belongings, or shut off utilities if rent goes unpaid. Maryland law does not permit this regardless of what the lease says — evicting a tenant always requires going through District Court and, ultimately, a warrant of restitution executed by the sheriff. A clause promising self-help eviction is unenforceable and can expose the landlord to liability if acted on.

Use instead: A clear statement of the landlord's right to pursue eviction through the courts for nonpayment or lease violations, consistent with Maryland's Failure to Pay Rent and Breach of Lease procedures.

2. Late Fees That Exceed Maryland's Statutory Cap

Maryland law limits how much a landlord can charge as a late fee on residential rent. Leases that set a flat late fee well above the statutory limit, or that compound daily without a cap, risk having that provision struck or renegotiated by a court — and can undermine the landlord's credibility on the rest of the case.

Use instead: A late fee clause that tracks the current statutory limit, reviewed periodically since these figures can be adjusted by the legislature.

3. Blanket Waivers of the Security Deposit Statute

Some leases attempt to have tenants waive their rights under Maryland's security deposit law — the 45-day return deadline, the itemized deduction list, or the right to a move-in inspection. These waivers are generally unenforceable; Maryland's security deposit statute is designed to protect tenants regardless of what the lease says, and a landlord who relies on a waiver clause instead of actually following the statute is exposed to the statute's penalties, including potential treble damages.

Use instead: A security deposit clause that mirrors the actual statutory process rather than attempting to opt out of it.

4. "As-Is" Clauses That Waive Habitability Obligations

A clause stating the tenant accepts the unit "as-is" and waives any claim related to condition or repairs does not override a landlord's underlying habitability obligations under Maryland law. Habitability and code-compliance duties exist independent of what the lease says, and an as-is clause can create a false sense of security that leaves a landlord unprepared when a habitability or rent escrow claim is filed.

Use instead: A clause documenting the unit's condition at move-in (ideally supported by a signed inspection checklist and photos) rather than a blanket waiver.

5. Confession of Judgment Clauses

Confession of judgment clauses — where a tenant agrees in advance to let the landlord obtain a judgment without a hearing — are restricted or unenforceable in Maryland residential leases. A landlord relying on this kind of clause to skip the normal court process is likely to have that portion of the lease disregarded, and may need to start the standard eviction process from scratch.

Use instead: Standard lease default and remedies language that assumes the normal District Court process will apply.

The Takeaway

A lease pulled from a generic template is often the biggest liability a landlord doesn't know they have. Having a Maryland-specific lease reviewed once, before there's a dispute, is far cheaper than discovering an unenforceable clause in the middle of a court case.

Of all the ways a Maryland landlord can end up owing a tenant money, security deposit mistakes are among the most common — and among the most avoidable. Maryland's security deposit law is specific and unforgiving of shortcuts, and the penalty for getting it wrong isn't just returning the deposit. It can mean paying up to three times the amount wrongfully withheld, plus the tenant's attorney's fees. Most landlords who end up facing treble damages didn't do anything deliberately wrong — they just didn't follow the statute's process closely enough.

The 45-Day Deadline

Once a tenancy ends, a Maryland landlord generally has 45 days to either return the security deposit in full or send the tenant a written, itemized list of deductions along with the balance of the deposit. Missing this window — even by a few days, even because a forwarding address was unclear — is one of the most common ways landlords lose the ability to make any deductions at all, regardless of how legitimate the damage was.

The Itemized List Requirement

It isn't enough to simply withhold part of the deposit for damage. Maryland law requires a written, itemized list showing exactly what was deducted and why. A landlord who sends a check for a reduced amount without an itemized breakdown — even with a good-faith basis for the deduction — has failed to comply with the statute as written, which is exactly the kind of technical miss that leads to a tenant's claim succeeding.

Move-In Inspection Requirements

Maryland law also requires landlords renting to give tenants notice of their right to a move-in inspection, and to actually document the unit's condition. A landlord who never gave this notice, or who has no documentation of the unit's condition at move-in, is in a weak position to later argue that damage at move-out was caused by the tenant rather than pre-existing.

Commingling and Interest Requirements

Depending on the size of the deposit and the county, Maryland law may require the deposit to be held in an escrow-type account and, in some cases, to accrue interest payable to the tenant at the end of the tenancy. Landlords who treat a security deposit as general operating cash, without tracking it separately, can run into compliance problems that have nothing to do with whether the deduction itself was fair.

How This Turns Into Treble Damages

Maryland's security deposit statute allows a tenant to sue for up to three times the amount of the deposit wrongfully withheld, plus attorney's fees, when a landlord fails to comply with the statute's procedural requirements — separate from any argument about whether the underlying deduction was reasonable. In practice, this means a landlord can have a completely legitimate claim for damage and still end up owing treble damages, simply because the itemized notice went out on day 50 instead of day 45, or wasn't itemized at all.

The Takeaway

Security deposit disputes are rarely won or lost on whether the damage was real. They're won or lost on whether the landlord followed the statute's process exactly. A short compliance review of your move-out procedure — before you're facing a departing tenant — is usually all it takes to avoid this entirely.

What Is a Rent Escrow Action in Maryland — and How Do Landlords Defend One?

Word count · ~900
Status · Draft, pending Stephen's review

Rent escrow is one of the more misunderstood tools in Maryland landlord-tenant law — and one that catches landlords off guard because it flips the usual dynamic. Instead of a landlord suing a tenant for unpaid rent, rent escrow lets a tenant proactively bring rent to the court instead of the landlord, on the theory that serious defects in the unit justify withholding payment. Understanding how it works — and how to defend against it — matters for any landlord managing rental property in Maryland.

What Rent Escrow Actually Is

Rent escrow is a formal District Court proceeding. Rather than simply refusing to pay rent (which exposes a tenant to a Failure to Pay Rent action), a tenant can ask the court's permission to pay rent into an escrow account instead of to the landlord, based on an allegation that the property has serious defects affecting health or safety that the landlord failed to fix after proper notice.

What Has to Happen Before a Tenant Can File

Generally, a tenant has to show that the landlord was given notice of the defect and a reasonable opportunity to repair it before escrow is appropriate. This notice requirement is one of the landlord's strongest points of defense — if a landlord was never properly notified, or wasn't given a fair chance to fix the issue, that alone can defeat the action.

Common Landlord Defenses

  • Improper or no notice. The tenant never gave the landlord a real opportunity to address the issue before filing.
  • The condition isn't serious enough. Rent escrow is intended for conditions that materially affect health or safety, not minor or cosmetic issues.
  • Repairs were already made. If the landlord addressed the issue before the hearing, that significantly weakens the tenant's position.
  • The tenant caused the condition. Damage caused by the tenant's own conduct is not a basis for withholding rent.
  • Access was denied. A landlord who was blocked from entering to make repairs has a strong defense.

What Happens If the Landlord Loses

If the court finds the conditions were serious and the landlord failed to act, it has a range of options: ordering repairs within a set timeframe, reducing the rent owed until repairs are made, authorizing the tenant to make repairs and deduct the cost, or releasing escrowed funds to the tenant. None of these outcomes are automatic — they depend heavily on the specific facts and how well-documented each side's position is.

Why This Matters Beyond the Single Case

A rent escrow finding against a landlord can also affect a later eviction case if the landlord tries to pursue Failure to Pay Rent for the same withheld amount — the two proceedings are closely linked. Landlords who treat an escrow filing as a minor annoyance rather than a serious legal matter often find themselves in a weaker position months later when they try to move forward with an eviction.

The Takeaway

Rent escrow actions are won or lost on documentation: what notice the tenant gave, what response the landlord made, and how quickly repairs happened once the landlord knew about the problem. Landlords who respond to repair requests in writing and keep records are almost always in a stronger position than those who don't.

Here's what landlords should understand about how a legal dispute translates into a tax question.

Unpaid Rent Generally Isn't a Deduction — Here's Why

Most individual landlords report rental income on a cash basis, meaning income is only recognized when it's actually received. If a tenant never pays rent, that unpaid rent was never reported as income in the first place — so there's typically nothing to "write off," because nothing was ever recognized as income to begin with. This surprises a lot of landlords who expect a bad-debt deduction similar to what a business on the accrual method might claim. The practical tax impact of unpaid rent is usually about what didn't happen (income that was never recognized), not a deduction that needs to be claimed.

What Actually Is Deductible

While the unpaid rent itself generally isn't deductible for a cash-basis landlord, the costs of pursuing the matter often are. Court filing fees, attorney's fees related to the eviction or lease dispute, and the cost of repairing property damage left by a tenant are generally treated as ordinary and necessary expenses of operating a rental property, and are deductible in the year they're paid. Landlords who don't track these costs separately often miss deductions they were actually entitled to take.

Judgments, Settlements, and How They're Treated

A judgment awarded to a landlord for unpaid rent generally isn't taxable income until it's actually collected — collecting on a judgment can be its own separate process from winning one. A settlement reached with a tenant should be evaluated based on what it's compensating for (unpaid rent versus property damage versus something else), because different components can be treated differently for tax purposes.

Entity Structure Changes the Whole Picture

Whether a rental property is held personally or through an LLC affects far more than liability protection — it affects how legal costs, judgments, and losses from a landlord-tenant dispute flow through to the owner's tax return, and how much exposure the owner has beyond the property itself. Landlords building or expanding a portfolio, or landlords who've had a bad experience with a difficult tenant, often ask about restructuring ownership at exactly the moment a legal dispute has made the stakes visible — which is also the moment when a rushed decision is most likely to create tax consequences no one planned for.

Why This Rarely Gets Addressed Properly

A landlord-tenant attorney who isn't also a CPA will typically resolve the legal matter and stop there — the tax question either doesn't come up or gets referred out to a separate accountant who never saw the underlying case and doesn't know which costs were actually incurred. A CPA who isn't also handling the legal side has the opposite gap — they see the tax return but not the legal history behind a judgment or settlement. Landlords end up explaining the same situation twice to two different professionals who never talk to each other.

The Takeaway

A landlord-tenant matter and its tax consequences are really one continuous situation, not two separate ones. Having both the legal and the accounting side handled by the same person — someone who saw the case from filing to judgment and understands exactly what it means for the return — closes a gap that most landlords don't realize exists until it costs them something.

IRS Collection Actions in Maryland: Audits, Liens, Levies, and Wage Garnishment Explained

Tax Law (Civil & Criminal) — new service line, confirmed Sept 2026
Target keyword · IRS tax attorney Maryland
Word count · ~1,000
Status · Draft, pending Stephen's review

An IRS collection notice rarely arrives with an explanation of what happens next. For most people, the process is a black box until a lien shows up on a credit report, a bank account is frozen, or a paycheck comes in short. Understanding the sequence — and where it can still be interrupted — makes a real difference in how a tax problem gets resolved.

It Usually Starts With an Audit or an Unfiled Return

IRS collection action generally traces back to one of two starting points: an audit that results in an assessed balance, or a return that was never filed, which the IRS eventually assesses on its own using limited information (often overstating what's actually owed). Either way, once a balance is assessed and unpaid, the IRS moves through a series of escalating collection tools — each one with its own notice requirements and, importantly, its own point at which a taxpayer can still act.

The Federal Tax Lien

A federal tax lien is the government's legal claim against a taxpayer's property — real estate, vehicles, financial accounts — securing the amount owed. A lien doesn't seize anything by itself, but it attaches to property and can significantly complicate selling a home, refinancing, or obtaining credit. Liens are public record, which is part of why they carry real financial and reputational weight beyond the dollar amount involved.

The Levy: When the IRS Actually Takes Property

A levy is different from a lien — it's the actual seizure of property or funds: a bank account levy, seizure of a vehicle, or garnishment of wages. The IRS is required to send a Final Notice of Intent to Levy and give the taxpayer a window to respond before a levy takes effect. That window is the single most important opportunity to act — once funds are levied from an account, getting them back is far harder than preventing the levy in the first place.

Wage Garnishment

An IRS wage garnishment (technically a continuous levy on wages) instructs an employer to withhold a portion of every paycheck until a balance is satisfied, released, or resolved through an agreement. Unlike many creditor garnishments, the IRS doesn't need a court judgment first, and the amount exempted from garnishment is often smaller than people expect. Employers are legally required to comply once the notice is received, which is why acting before that notice arrives — not after — is what actually changes the outcome.

Two Ways to Resolve an Outstanding Balance

  • Installment agreements — a structured monthly payment plan that stops active enforcement (liens and levies) as long as payments stay current, though interest and penalties generally continue to accrue on the remaining balance.
  • Offers in compromise — an agreement to settle a tax debt for less than the full amount owed, available when the IRS agrees the full balance is genuinely not collectible given the taxpayer's income, expenses, and asset equity. Offers in compromise are frequently advertised as a quick fix; in practice, the IRS accepts a minority of offers, and the application itself requires detailed and accurate financial disclosure.

When It Becomes a Criminal Matter

Most IRS collection cases are entirely civil. They become criminal exposure in narrower circumstances — willful failure to file over multiple years, deliberate underreporting, or affirmative acts to conceal income or assets. The line between an aggressive but lawful civil dispute and a criminal referral often comes down to intent and pattern, which is exactly the kind of judgment call that benefits from legal representation early, before a civil audit becomes something more serious.

The Takeaway

Every stage of IRS collection — audit, lien, levy, garnishment — comes with a notice and a window to respond. The taxpayers who end up in the worst position are usually the ones who didn't act during that window, not the ones who owed the most. Representation before a levy or garnishment takes effect is almost always more effective than trying to unwind one after the fact.

LLC vs. Sole Proprietorship in Maryland: Legal and Liability Considerations for New Business Owners

Most new Maryland business owners default to operating as a sole proprietorship simply because it requires no setup — there's no formation filing, no separate entity to maintain. That simplicity comes at a cost that often isn't visible until something goes wrong: a lawsuit, an unpaid business debt, or an injury on business premises. Choosing between a sole proprietorship and an LLC is really a decision about where that risk lands.

The Core Difference: Where Liability Stops

A sole proprietorship has no legal separation between the owner and the business. If the business is sued or owes money it can't pay, the owner's personal assets — a home, personal savings, a personal vehicle — are exposed. An LLC (limited liability company) creates a distinct legal entity. Properly formed and maintained, it generally shields the owner's personal assets from the business's debts and liabilities, limiting exposure to what's been invested in the business itself.

"Properly Formed and Maintained" Is Doing a Lot of Work

The liability protection an LLC offers isn't automatic just because articles of organization were filed with the state. Courts can disregard the LLC's separate status — a doctrine sometimes called "piercing the corporate veil" — when the owner treats the LLC as an extension of themselves: mixing personal and business funds, failing to maintain separate records, or undercapitalizing the business relative to its obvious risks. An LLC that exists only on paper doesn't protect much.

Taxes: Where the Two Structures Often Look the Same

By default, a single-member LLC is taxed identically to a sole proprietorship — income and expenses flow through to the owner's personal return, and there's no separate business tax return required. This surprises a lot of new business owners who assume forming an LLC automatically changes how they're taxed. It doesn't, unless the LLC elects a different tax treatment (S-corporation status, for example), which is a separate decision with its own tradeoffs around self-employment tax and payroll requirements.

Quick Comparison

Sole ProprietorshipLLC
Personal liabilityUnlimited — no separation from the ownerGenerally limited to the business, if properly maintained
FormationNone requiredArticles of organization filed with the state, ongoing compliance

When the Decision Matters Most

  • The business involves physical premises, equipment, or activities with injury risk
  • The business takes on debt, signs leases, or enters contracts in its own name
  • There's more than one owner, where an operating agreement becomes essential to avoid future disputes
  • The owner has significant personal assets they want insulated from business risk

The Takeaway

The choice between a sole proprietorship and an LLC is a liability decision first and a tax decision second — and the two are easy to conflate. Getting the entity structure right at the start, with attention to both sides, is far cheaper than restructuring after a dispute has already put personal assets at risk.

Chapter 7 vs. Chapter 13 Bankruptcy in Maryland: Which Path Fits Your Situation

Personal and Business Bankruptcy — new service line, confirmed Sept 2026
Target keyword · bankruptcy attorney Maryland
Word count · ~950
Status · Draft, pending Stephen's review

For most individuals considering bankruptcy in Maryland, the decision comes down to two chapters of the U.S. Bankruptcy Code: Chapter 7 and Chapter 13. They solve the same underlying problem — unmanageable debt — through very different processes, and the wrong choice can mean losing property that could have been protected, or committing to years of payments that weren't actually necessary.

Chapter 7: Liquidation

Chapter 7 is often called "liquidation" bankruptcy, though in practice most individual filers don't lose significant property — Maryland and federal exemptions protect a meaningful amount of equity in a home, vehicle, and personal belongings. A bankruptcy trustee reviews the filer's assets, sells anything that isn't exempt to pay creditors, and most remaining unsecured debt (credit cards, medical bills, personal loans) is discharged. The process is comparatively fast, often concluding within a few months.

Qualifying for Chapter 7: The Means Test

Not everyone qualifies for Chapter 7. Filers must pass a "means test" comparing household income to the Maryland median for a household of the same size. Falling below the median generally qualifies a filer automatically; income above the median requires a more detailed calculation of disposable income after allowed expenses. Filers who don't pass the means test aren't shut out of bankruptcy relief — they're generally directed toward Chapter 13 instead.

Chapter 13: Reorganization

Chapter 13 is a repayment plan, typically spanning three to five years, built around the filer's actual income and expenses. It's often the better fit for someone who doesn't qualify for Chapter 7, who has non-exempt property they want to keep (like significant home equity), or who is behind on a mortgage and wants a structured way to catch up while keeping the house. At the end of a successful Chapter 13 plan, remaining eligible unsecured debt is discharged.

Business Bankruptcy

Business owners facing debt they can't manage have their own set of options, which can include Chapter 7 liquidation of a business entity, or reorganization under Chapter 11 (or, for smaller businesses, a streamlined subchapter of Chapter 11) aimed at keeping the business operating while restructuring its debts. The right path depends heavily on whether the business itself is viable going forward, or whether the goal is an orderly wind-down.

What Bankruptcy Doesn't Erase

Certain debts generally survive bankruptcy regardless of chapter, including most tax debt (with narrow exceptions based on how old the debt is and whether returns were filed on time), recent domestic support obligations, and most student loans absent a separate, harder-to-obtain showing of hardship. Understanding what actually gets discharged — and what doesn't — is often the difference between bankruptcy solving the problem and simply delaying it.

The Tax Side of Bankruptcy

Bankruptcy and tax law intersect more than most filers expect. Discharged debt can sometimes trigger tax consequences outside of bankruptcy (though debts discharged in bankruptcy itself are generally excluded from taxable income), and unresolved tax debt is one of the more common complications in a bankruptcy filing. Evaluating a bankruptcy filing without also looking at the filer's tax situation can miss real problems — or real opportunities — that a purely legal review wouldn't catch.

The Takeaway

Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 solve different problems for different situations — the right choice depends on income, what property is at stake, and what's actually driving the debt. A short conversation before filing is usually enough to identify which path actually fits.
